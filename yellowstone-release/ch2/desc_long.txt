March 1894, Pelican Valley, deep in Yellowstone's backcountry. An Army scout follows a trail of snowshoe tracks through the snow and finds Ed Howell, the most notorious poacher in the park, bent over a dead bison. Howell had killed at least 11 of Yellowstone's last wild bison that winter, and there was almost no law to punish him. Within two months that changed, and the reason is one of the strangest chapters in the history of America's parks: for 32 years, Yellowstone was run by the United States Army.

In this episode: the photographs and sketches that showed Congress a place that looked unreal; the campfire story that probably never happened; the first superintendent, who wasn't paid at all; the lawless years; Captain Moses Harris and 50 men of the First U.S. Cavalry riding into Mammoth in 1886; the soldiers whose geyser talks began what rangers still do today; ski patrols, Fort Yellowstone and the Buffalo Soldiers' bicycles; the capture that led to the 1894 law protecting the park's animals; the 1908 stagecoach hold-up (17 coaches by the most-cited count, and the robber was never caught) and the 1914 robbery photographed in progress; and how soldiers became the first park rangers.

Filmed on the ground by us in October 2021: Mammoth Hot Springs and Fort Yellowstone (still the park's headquarters), the Roosevelt Arch, the Grand Canyon of the Yellowstone, the Firehole River and West Thumb.
