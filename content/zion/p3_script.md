## THE PAINTINGS NOBODY BELIEVED (~1:30)

[VIS: OWN slickrock + canyon cliffs (a UNUSED own clip, e.g. 8aec6b1943ed Left Fork red slickrock), static]

For most of the nineteenth century, Zion Canyon was known mostly to the farming towns of southern Utah. To most of the country, it was a rumor.

[VIS: PD — Frederick Dellenbaugh's oil "Zion Canyon" c.1903 (NPGallery ZION 38105), slow pan across the paint]

In the summer of nineteen oh three, a painter named Frederick Dellenbaugh came to see it. He was no stranger to the West. As a young man he had traveled with John Wesley Powell, and he had seen a lot of canyons. This one stopped him. In his words, never before had such a naked mountain of rock entered our minds.

[VIS: PD — Dellenbaugh album detail / second painting from the NPGallery album, slow zoom]

He painted it. His canvases of Zion were shown at the nineteen oh four World's Fair in St. Louis, and he wrote about the canyon for Scribner's Magazine, calling it a new valley of wonders. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — a crowded 1904 exhibition hall, visitors in long coats standing before a huge canvas of red cliffs, faces turned away]

According to the National Park Service, the visitors who saw those paintings in St. Louis doubted that a place like that could be real. It was a hard thing to prove. There was no highway to the canyon, and for most people, the paint was the only proof.

## A PARK BY PETITION (~3:20)

[VIS: OWN Zion Canyon view, own_al_1110165344 (Angels Landing summit view, slow zoom)]

Back in Utah, people who actually lived near it started asking for official protection.

[VIS: PD — USGS/NPS archival photo of early surveyors or horse party in Zion Canyon (check credit line)]

In nineteen oh eight, eight ranchers from southern Utah asked the government for a land survey. The General Land Office sent a surveyor named Leo Snow. He surveyed the canyon and wrote that it was surpassed in grandeur only by the view of the Grand Canyon of the Colorado. His recommendation was short: Zion Canyon, he said, ought to be made a national park.

[VIS: PD — NPS photo "Zion Canyon, 1928" or similar mid-canyon view (z2 already used) — use a NEW Nov-1909 era image if one is found; else ANIM]

On July thirty-first, nineteen oh nine, President William Howard Taft signed a proclamation that created Mukuntuweap National Monument. It was small, about fifteen thousand acres, a strip roughly half a mile wide and eight miles long. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — a hand-lettered map being redrawn, the boundary lines widening]

The name didn't last. It was unpopular with local people, and in nineteen eighteen the monument was renamed Zion and enlarged to more than seventy-six thousand acres.

[VIS: PD — LOC or NPS document page, "Zion National Park, Utah, Act of November 19, 1919" (check rights line)]

And on November nineteenth, nineteen nineteen, Congress made Zion a national park, Utah's first, signed by President Woodrow Wilson.

But a park on paper is not a park. In nineteen twenty, about thirty-seven hundred people visited Zion. There was a dirt road, a tent camp, and no way to get here that wasn't hard. ⟨2.0⟩

## THE RAILROAD AND THE PRESIDENT (~5:10)

[VIS: ANIM — a railroad map of Utah, a line of track reaching a small desert town]

Some of the people who saw the opportunity were railroad men. Union Pacific and its subsidiary, the Utah Parks Company, wanted tourists to ride their trains to the western parks. A rail spur reached Cedar City, Utah, in nineteen twenty-three, and one historian counts about one point seven million dollars spent on improvements in nineteen twenty-three and twenty-four.

[VIS: ANIM — horse riders in a line climbing a canyon road in dust, seen from behind; one in a wide cowboy hat]

In June nineteen twenty-three, the biggest tourist of all arrived. President Warren Harding came to Cedar City, Utah, by train, to thousands of cheering locals. Sheriffs rounded up about seventy-five horses to ride him up the canyon. According to a Deseret News account, the president wore a cowboy hat, a kerchief and a pair of oversized chaps. His wife, Florence, said she wouldn't have missed the trip for anything. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — a quiet hospital hallway at dusk; a drawn curtain; no faces]

Five weeks later, Harding was dead. He died in San Francisco on August second, still on the trip. But the visit had done its work. Zion was famous.

[VIS: PD — NPS photo of the Utah Parks Company bus / early tourists (reuse only if not used in Part 2; else new photo)]

Visitors came by train and by bus. By nineteen thirty, nearly fifty-five thousand people visited in a year, about fifteen times as many as ten years earlier.

And that created a new problem. ⟨1.5⟩

## THE DEAD END (~6:45)

[VIS: OWN overhang with varnish streaks (e04023e3df6d is Part 2 — use an UNUSED own clip, e.g. 6fee62373944 upper Narrows path)]

Zion Canyon is a box canyon. The road went in, and the road came out the same way.

[VIS: ANIM — a map: Zion at the left, Bryce Canyon and the Grand Canyon's North Rim to the east, a long winding dashed route through Hurricane and Kanab]

Bryce Canyon and the other wonders of the plateau country lay beyond the cliff wall. To drive from Zion to Bryce, you had to go the long way around, a hundred and forty-nine miles.

[VIS: PD — NPS photo "Cableworks above Zion Canyon" (only if cleared) or ANIM of the wire and the lumber bundle]

There was an older way over that wall, and you have already heard about it. Around nineteen hundred, a logger named David Flanigan strung about fifty thousand feet of wire up the east rim to lower lumber into the canyon. Before that, a wagon trip to bring lumber down could take a week or longer.

[VIS: ANIM — a small brown-and-white farm dog riding a tiny crate down a long wire, canyon far below, in a gentle storybook style]

And according to the Park Service, a family dog named Darkey once rode that wire two thousand feet down the mountain. ⟨2.0⟩

A dog could do it. A car could not.

## THE IMPOSSIBLE TUNNEL (~8:10)

[VIS: PD — LOC HAER UT-0271 Zion–Mount Carmel Highway photo (verify rights line) or NPS aerial of the switchbacks]

So the Park Service, the state of Utah and the Bureau of Public Roads agreed to build something that sounded almost absurd: a road up the side of the canyon, and a tunnel through the cliff above it, to the plateau.

[VIS: ANIM — a surveyor with a transit on a bare ledge, rope around his waist, red cliffs above]

It took more than four years of surveys just to find a route. A local stockman, John Winder, is credited with showing the surveyors the way up Pine Creek. When they were done, the road to Bryce Canyon would shrink from a hundred and forty-nine miles to eighty-eight. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: PD — NPS photo "Blasting gallery at the large tunnel" (ZION 10190) or ANIM]

Construction began in September nineteen twenty-seven. A Nevada construction company from Fallon took the job. About two hundred workers and their families lived in a camp near the work.

[VIS: ANIM — a hole blasted in a cliff face high above the canyon, a window with daylight pouring in]

The crews started by blasting openings in the cliff face, windows high above Pine Creek. Through them, they hauled out the rock and let in air and light. Then they bored the tunnel in two directions, toward each other, and widened it with dynamite and air shovels. Several times, parts of it collapsed. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — night, a single bulb in a rock tunnel, dust drifting through the beam]

The work was dangerous. One local history names two men who died: a crew boss, Johnny Morrison, and a man named Mac McClain. The Park Service pages don't mention deaths, so we say it carefully: at least two men reportedly died building this road.

[VIS: OWN canyon / slot walls (4c4a6e310ccb pillar, 3 s, or 626e3bf0851b Narrows deep slot, walking), slow]

When the two bores met, a newspaper reported, they were nowhere more than three inches out of line. After a mile of rock, that was close enough to shake hands. ⟨2.0⟩

## THE FOURTH OF JULY (~10:45)

[VIS: ANIM — a crowd of cars and people on a canyon road, flags in the wind, a radio microphone on a stand, no faces]

The tunnel was dedicated on July fourth, nineteen thirty. Utah's governor, George Dern, was there. A prayer was offered by the president of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, Heber J. Grant. Visiting governors from a governors' conference came to see it, and the ceremony was broadcast live on the radio.

[VIS: PD — NPS photo, tunnel gallery windows from the canyon floor (ZION 13572 is used; try tunnel-era photos from the lead list)]

By the numbers, it was one point one mile long, twenty-two feet wide and sixteen feet high, with six windows cut into the cliff. The tunnel alone had cost about half a million dollars. The whole highway cost closer to two million, at the start of the Great Depression. At the time, the National Park Service called it the longest vehicular tunnel in the national park system.

[VIS: OWN overhang + cliffs from the car or the trail (a clip not used before)]

It worked. Zion was no longer a dead end. Before the tunnel, the park had about a thousand visitors a day. It was about to see a lot more.

## THE TUNNEL TODAY (~11:50)

[VIS: PD — NPS photo of the tunnel, a recent date, a wide shot (credit line)]

The tunnel is still there, and it is still in use. In two thousand twelve, the American Society of Civil Engineers named it a historic civil engineering landmark.

[VIS: ANIM — one of the six gallery windows from inside, a sealed rockslide, closed by a boulder wall; a drifting leaf]

Not everything has lasted. One of the windows was closed because seepage turned the road to ice. Another was sealed by a rockslide in nineteen fifty-eight, and two more were closed later. The same mountain that the tunnel crosses keeps working on it, which is the same story as the lakebed, the lodge and the trail: this canyon is always moving.

[VIS: PD — NPS photo, a large vehicle in the tunnel with a ranger directing traffic]

And because it was built for nineteen-twenties cars, today's biggest vehicles can't squeeze through alongside traffic. Anything over eleven feet four inches tall, or seven feet ten wide, needs a permit and one-way traffic control.
