## COLD OPEN — "About a Minute" (0:00)

[VIS: OWN Zion Lodge lawn + lodge buildings (137e3a27283d, t0-15 ONLY), slow push]

The hotel in the middle of Zion Canyon sits on the bottom of a lake, a lake that vanished more than four thousand years ago.

[VIS: ANIM — the same canyon, cliffs unchanged, the floor under deep still water]

Where this lawn is now, the water once stood about three hundred and fifty to three hundred and eighty feet deep. ⟨1.5⟩

And that lake was made in about a minute.

[VIS: ANIM — a vast section of canyon wall breaking away, dust rising]

About four thousand eight hundred years ago, a huge part of the canyon wall near a peak called the Sentinel broke loose and fell into Zion Canyon. Models suggest it reached nearly two hundred miles per hour. One of the geologists who studied it put it simply: by a minute, it was pretty much done. ⟨2.0⟩

[VIS: ANIM — river backing up behind a wall of rubble, water spreading up the canyon]

The rubble dammed the Virgin River, and behind it grew a lake that some call Lake Zion. It lasted about seven hundred years. When it finally filled with sand and mud, it left the flat green valley floor that millions of visitors now walk, ride and sleep on.

[VIS: OWN Angels Landing summit photo looking down the canyon (own_al_1110172455), slow zoom out]

The same geologist called it a minute with up to ten thousand years of consequences.

And the mountain isn't finished. In nineteen ninety-five, part of that ancient slide moved again, and about four hundred and thirty people were cut off at the lodge. ⟨1.5⟩

This is America's National Parks: The Hidden History. Zion, Part Two. The Day the Mountain Fell.

## A MOUNTAIN LET GO (~1:42)

[VIS: OWN slickrock + Zion Canyon cliffs view (93952cead405), static]

The walls of Zion Canyon rise two to three thousand feet above the canyon floor. The Park Service counts them among the highest sandstone cliffs on Earth.

[VIS: OWN overhang with desert-varnish streaks, cliffs behind (4a56def9f77b)]

But in the middle of the canyon, the floor goes strangely flat, wide enough for meadows, a road and a hotel. In twenty sixteen, a team from the University of Utah published the explanation.

[VIS: ANIM — a geologist sampling a boulder; diagram of cosmic rays striking rock]

They dated the slide using beryllium-ten, a rare form of the element that slowly builds up in rock exposed to cosmic rays. Measured in boulders the slide left behind, it gave an age of about four thousand eight hundred years, give or take four hundred.

[VIS: ANIM — side view of the collapse, the rock mass crossing the canyon]

What fell was enormous: about ten billion cubic feet of rock. To rebuild that missing mountainside, the researchers said, you would need ninety times the concrete in Hoover Dam.

[VIS: graphic — scale bar, "about 5x the largest historic non-volcanic landslides in North America"]

It was roughly five times bigger than the largest landslides in North America's recorded history, not counting volcanoes. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — debris fanning out across the canyon floor, dust cloud]

Models suggest it averaged about a hundred and twelve miles per hour. The main mass crossed the canyon in about twenty seconds. The debris kept spreading for about thirty more.

When the dust settled, the canyon was blocked from wall to wall by a dam about two miles long and, in places, about six hundred and fifty feet thick.

## A LAKE IN THE DESERT (~3:23)

[VIS: ANIM — the river pooling behind the dam, water climbing the cliffs]

The Virgin River had nowhere to go. It backed up behind the rubble, and the canyon began to fill. The researchers estimate the lake reached its first high point within about five to ten years.

[VIS: OWN Angels Landing summit photo looking down the canyon (own_al_1110175048), ANIM water overlay rising on the valley floor]

Picture this view as water. At its highest, the lake covered almost two and a half square miles and reached almost to the Narrows. Over the spot where Zion Lodge stands today, it was about three hundred and fifty to three hundred and eighty feet deep.

[VIS: ANIM — water spilling over the crest of the dam, the level dropping]

Then the water found a way over the top. The lake cut into the crest of its dam and dropped to a lower, steadier level. It shrank to about one point two square miles, but it still reached more than four miles up the canyon, to the Temple of Sinawava.

[VIS: OWN Narrows: river between walls, autumn leaves (210759a38d30, t25-34)]

And then, slowly, the river buried its own lake. Year after year it carried sand and mud down the canyon and dropped them in the still water.

[VIS: ANIM — time-lapse: the lake shrinking as green flats spread across it]

About seven hundred years after the mountain fell, around four thousand one hundred years ago, the lake was full. Not of water. Of sediment. ⟨2.0⟩

[VIS: OWN deer grazing among cottonwoods (4ec0463298de)]

That is the flat floor of Zion Canyon. The meadows, the cottonwoods, the road and the lodge lawn all sit on a lake that filled itself in. Jeff Moore, one of the University of Utah geologists behind the study, called it creating paradise through cataclysm.

[VIS: OWN Narrows: walls, river, boulders (d20e7c4997eb)]

The river has been eating away at the dam ever since. The researchers estimate that about forty-five percent of the slide's debris is already gone, carried off by the Virgin River.

## THE LODGE ON THE LAKEBED (~5:25)

[VIS: OWN trail under overhanging alcove, autumn trees, railing (10ab2d0141c6)]

Thousands of years later, people found that flat floor useful.

[VIS: ANIM — 1917: a dirt road and a row of canvas tent cabins under the cliffs]

In nineteen seventeen, the first road for cars reached deep into Zion Canyon, and a tent camp opened on the old lakebed. It had ten tent cabins, right where the lodge stands today.

[VIS: ANIM — 1920s steam train arriving at a small desert station]

In the early nineteen twenties, the Union Pacific Railroad saw a business opportunity. A rail spur reached Cedar City, Utah, in nineteen twenty-three, and the railroad's Utah Parks Company needed a hotel inside the canyon. The company hired Gilbert Stanley Underwood, an architect who designed lodges in several national parks. Zion Lodge was finished in May nineteen twenty-five.

[VIS: ANIM — a bundle of lumber gliding down a long wire from a cliff top to the canyon floor]

Here's the detail we love. The lodge was built with two hundred and sixty-five thousand board feet of lumber, brought down from the plateau on a wire. A tramway called the Cable Mountain Draw Works lowered it more than two thousand vertical feet, down the cliff to the canyon floor.

Hold on to that name, Cable Mountain. We'll come back to it. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — night, the main lodge building in flames]

For more than forty years, Underwood's lodge stood on the lakebed. Then, on January twenty-sixth, nineteen sixty-six, the main lodge building burned.

[VIS: ANIM — workers raising a plain, boxy prefabricated building]

The replacement went up fast. A new, prefabricated building was operating just a hundred and eight days after the fire.

[VIS: OWN Zion Lodge lawn + lodge buildings (137e3a27283d, t0-15 ONLY), different framing from the cold open]

In nineteen ninety, the lodge was restored to look more like Underwood's original design. That's the lodge you see today, standing in the middle of the old lake.

And in nineteen ninety-five, it became an island.

## THE NIGHT THE SENTINEL MOVED (~7:20)

[VIS: ANIM — night, the canyon road along the river, a slope beginning to slump]

On the night of April twelfth, nineteen ninety-five, part of the old Sentinel slide moved again.

[VIS: ANIM — earth and rock sliding into the river, the water blocked]

A landslide more than five hundred feet long, over a hundred thousand cubic yards of rock and soil, slid into the Virgin River and blocked it. A small lake began to form behind it. And the river, pushed out of its bed, cut a new path straight through the road.

[VIS: PD — 1995 Superintendent's Annual Report page on the landslide (NPGallery ZION 15652), slow pan on the text]

Rangers estimated that six hundred feet of road was destroyed. It was the only road in and out of the canyon. Upstream, at Zion Lodge, about four hundred and thirty people, guests and employees, by the Park Service's count, were cut off. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: ANIM — floodlights, machines cutting a narrow track into the canyon's east wall]

Crews carved an emergency detour road out of the canyon's east wall. It took about twenty-two hours. On the night of April thirteenth, the people stuck at the lodge were finally able to leave.

[VIS: ANIM — muddy floodwater tearing at a road edge]

Three years later, the old slide was hit again. In nineteen ninety-eight, a flash flood sent the river from two hundred to four thousand five hundred cubic feet per second, and it damaged the scenic drive at the slide.

[VIS: OWN Narrows: towering walls, river (ff90f11838d6)]

The dam from four thousand eight hundred years ago is still there, and the river is still cutting into it.

## A CANYON STILL FALLING (~8:55)

[VIS: OWN tilt up red cliffs from alcove, rockfall boulders (4512fe47671e, t4-6 on the boulders)]

The Park Service puts it plainly. In Zion, rocks fall, frequently, and it is impossible to predict when or where the next one will happen.

[VIS: ANIM — water seeping into a crack, freezing, the crack widening]

Water seeps into cracks and freezes, wedging them apart. The sun heats the rock by day and it cools at night, expanding and shrinking until it fractures. Floods undercut the cliffs from below. And sometimes, as the park itself says, rocks just fall.

[VIS: OWN varnish-streaked overhang, autumn (e04023e3df6d)]

On August first, nineteen sixty-eight, about five thousand tons of rock came down at the end of the trail that leads to the Narrows.

[VIS: PD — NPS photo of the 2019 Cable Mountain rockfall and debris at Weeping Rock (check credit line)]

Then, on the evening of August twenty-fourth, twenty nineteen, a huge slab of sandstone broke off Cable Mountain, about three thousand feet above Weeping Rock. Yes, the same Cable Mountain that once lowered the lumber for the lodge.

More than four hundred and thirty-five thousand cubic feet of debris came down. Three visitors were hurt, and one was taken to the hospital. Part of the East Rim Trail was buried under what geologists classified as a small rock avalanche. ⟨1.5⟩

[VIS: OWN overhang + cliffs (b85b026e5212)]

The Weeping Rock Trail stayed closed for three years. It reopened in November twenty twenty-two. A year later, another slab fell near the same spot, and the trail closed again. It didn't reopen until September twenty twenty-five.

[VIS: OWN red cliffs from overhang (6e1d7e5f4d43)]

After that second fall, the park's superintendent, Jeff Bradybaugh, said it well: rockfall can happen at any time in this highly erosive landscape.

And yet, according to the Park Service, no visitor has ever been killed by a rockfall in Zion, though people have been hurt, and there have been close calls. ⟨2.0⟩

## LIFE ON THE OLD LAKEBED (~10:59)

[VIS: OWN mule deer in meadow (2fe1309ede17)]

Today, mule deer graze in the meadows on the old lakebed. But two Oregon State University ecologists argue that even this quiet scene has a hidden history.

[VIS: OWN three mule deer close (3aecad02bed0)]

By their count, in nineteen twenty-four, about eight thousand four hundred people visited Zion. Ten years later, almost sixty-nine thousand did. A Park Service report from nineteen thirty-nine said the crowds tended to drive out the cougars.

Without cougars, the researchers say, the canyon's deer boomed, from fewer than eighty in nineteen thirty to about six hundred by nineteen forty-two. Between nineteen thirty-eight and nineteen forty-seven, the Park Service killed or removed seven hundred and eighty deer from Zion Canyon.

[VIS: OWN deer (7fbc669a7bc8)]

And the deer ate the young cottonwoods. In Zion Canyon, the researchers found only about twenty-three cottonwoods per kilometer of stream that had taken root after nineteen forty. In nearby North Creek, where the cougars stayed, there were almost nine hundred. Without new trees holding the soil, the banks wore away.

It is the authors' hypothesis, and ideas like this are debated among ecologists. But it's a reminder that the lakebed is still changing too.

## CLOSER — "Paradise Through Cataclysm" (~12:25)

[VIS: OWN Angels Landing summit photo looking down the canyon (own_al_1110172905), slow pan]

So the next time you stand on the lawn at Zion Lodge, look down. You are standing on the mud and sand of a lake that some call Lake Zion, a lake born from a mountainside that fell in about a minute.

Could it happen again? Jeff Moore chose his words carefully: we have no evidence that something like this is imminent. But this canyon is still being built, one falling rock at a time. ⟨2.0⟩

[VIS: OWN overhang with cliffs, slow tilt (4a56def9f77b — 2nd use; swap if a better unused angle exists)]

Building anything in this canyon has always meant a fight with the rock. And the boldest fight of all was a road. In the late nineteen twenties, crews set out to blast a tunnel more than a mile long through a sandstone cliff.

How they did it, and how this canyon became a national park at all, is our final chapter.
